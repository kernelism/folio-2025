> The title is clickbait.

I don't know how the general populace works, but I have a deep, internal quest to become exceptionally good at something. More concretely, the quest is for recognition. I believe recognition comes with being good at something (too naive?). There have been many instances in life where I was really good at something but wasn't able to convert it into real success. This has left me really disappointed in myself for a long time.

Currently, I'm in the field of tech. I try to build cool software, including the one you're reading right now. For most of my journey in tech, I was under the assumption that I was terrible at building or designing software. Implicitly, this somehow helped me source the motivation to constantly work on it. I read more, built more, experimented more and never got tired of what I was doing. I knew it wasn't going to come to me easily.

And the result? I genuinely got better. More importantly, I acknowledged to myself that I was indeed better. My friends noticed it too. I was suddenly the one everyone wanted on their hackathon team; the one they could depend on to fix their merge conflicts, or even get Arch Linux set up on their machine. I have to admit it was pretty cool to be in this spot. I knew I was doing something right. Can you guess what followed?

*Complacency.*

I was no longer working my ass off like I used to. I wasn't trying to learn new stuff. I was no longer interested in building, reading or experimenting. I knew I was doing something wrong, but the spark wasn't there to act on that thought. I had decided, against my will, that whatever I knew so far was sufficient. I was no longer naturally motivated to do more. I genuinely think this is what differentiates people who go on to be extremely successful from the average Joe. They know when *not* to stop.

---

So, what's the workaround?

There is no single way to fix this. You have to find something that makes you subconsciously believe you're mid. Personally, I do this by exploring stuff I've never seen before. I am genuinely bad at building intuition for AI/ML research unless someone spoon-feeds me. So, I periodically read a really complex paper that makes me feel extremely stupid.

The result? I'm way more motivated to find out more about these things, and even about the things I think I'm good at. I'm more interested in reading books and articles, watching podcasts and even listening to random tech creators rambling on YouTube.

I know what you're thinking. This sounds a lot like Indian dad advice, where you're eternally pushed towards more. That's bad parenting. My advice is an internal way of training your mind to never be complacent. It only works if you consciously want to do more, but subconsciously find yourself uninterested in doing so. It always comes down to thinking you've done enough for the time being, but successful people push the goalpost further before they've even reached the current one.

Ultimately, the journey to mastery isn't linear. Complacency will always creep in, and your ego will try to convince you that your current level of skill is enough. The key is to deliberately put yourself in situations that expose your ignorance, to feel small, and to let that discomfort drive you forward. It's always you vs you.

Cheers!

*— Arjun*
