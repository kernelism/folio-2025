---
title: The pitfalls of convenience
date: 2026-09-30
theme: writing
---

I'm a software engineer in essence and have lived the Silicon Valley life. Everyone over there seems to think, believe and invest in enterprises that make things convenient. People (including myself) have scrambled for years to identify "pain-points" to solve, make easier, and make money from. It's definitely not a load of BS (or probably it is?).

I started thinking about this after looking at RAM prices these days. We could purchase entire computers a decade ago for how much RAM chips alone sell for right now. And for what? For people to communicate with a machine that probabilistically spits out linguistically coherent sentences. I know it has helped several people in several different ways, but what is it coming at the cost of? In my opinion, we're losing the opportunity to have authentic experiences in the name of convenience. We're forgetting that the very absurdity of reality is what makes it meaningful.

I think it all starts with an obsession for productivity. There's a new tool every day that attempts to save you some more time on top of all the time you're already saving. For instance, Anthropic now lets you prompt Claude through your phone. If I had a session running and had to context switch because, say, I had to take the bus, I can now continue on my phone. Honestly, cool feature if you keep traveling or like to stay locked in. The possibilities are endless. Does it really help us? 

What I've discovered after being a "productivity geek" for a while is that it does not make your days calmer. It does not make you feel like you have more time for everything. It absolutely does not stop the relentless pursuit of mastering time. There are an infinite number of things in the world we could be doing and only a finite amount of time. What this means is that there will always be more things you need to cram into your limited time. 

The only way to feel in control is to let go. We need to understand that it is impossible to get everything done. We need to understand that we don't use time, but time uses us. The industrialization and automation that never stops has led us to believe that automating something is always for the better. It's like asking an artist if they would deliver a mediocre product if that meant they'd always be super economically successful. A capitalist would absolutely agree, and they're the ones leading us to believe that life is all about automating every little inconvenience, so much that making sure you don't have inconvenience is itself an inconvenience. Life is more art than money, retirement and mastery over time. 

I'd like to reach into my bag for cash and not use Apple Pay. I'd like to interact with an actual human being and not use a kiosk to order food. I'd like to make the effort to call a taxi service to get a cab and not use my phone. I'd like to make photo scrapbooks and not post on Instagram. Isn't this what we live for? It's that small extra inconvenience that builds relationships, changes how society around you behaves and what expectations are like between human beings. 

Well again, I think my claims are also a bit dubious. I've conveniently stopped at a certain point where I think convenience and human interaction maintain a good balance (in my opinion). The extremist vision would look much more like the pre-industrial era. What if you just walked everywhere, did manual labor to make ends meet, and whatnot?

Nevertheless, my original claim stands. Mastering time is just logically impossible for 3 reasons. One, time is finite and the number of things to do is absolutely infinite. The employee who works the most constantly gets more work to do. Two, authentic experiences don't appear out of a planned calendar. Three, the instruments and constructs used to measure and quantify time are man-made. 

Let time use you to the fullest.
